Review by looking — design is the surface, requirements are not

You asked for a demo before a ticket closes, and to stop reviewing requirements. Stepping back, those are the same request: every decision you own should be made by looking at something, and right now three of the five are made by reading.

Your five decisions, and how each reaches youmeasured against the current rules
DecisionReaches you asHow you review it
Scope an issue (L0)A five-field issue draft, plus a demo of the current state if it exists mixed

The one that should worry us most is the last row

Closing a ticket is the moment you declare the thing you asked for actually exists. Right now nothing is shown to you at that moment — not a screen, not a demo, not a diff. The decision with the least evidence behind it is the one that ends the work.

This is the fourth time this pattern has come up, and ASKS.md already predicted it: "Show him the thing; don't describe the thing… one rule that keeps being rediscovered in a new context, which makes it an amendment candidate rather than a fourth independent statement of it." This proposal is that amendment — stated once, for all five decisions, instead of a fifth special case.

Why requirements are the wrong thing to send younot a preference — a role mismatch

REQUIREMENTS.md is written to be checkable by someone who wasn't in the conversation — that is its stated job. Its readers are the test engineer and the gate, and for them numbered, exhaustive, unambiguous prose is exactly right.

You are not that reader. You already know what you asked for. What you need to know is whether what got built looks like what you meant — a question prose answers badly and a screen answers instantly. Sending you the requirements is asking you to simulate a screen in your head from a list, which is slow, error-prone, and something the design artifact already does properly.

So: requirements don't go away, they stop being addressed to you

They are still written, still numbered, still testable — they are the contract the gate holds the work to, and removing them would remove the only thing that makes "built" checkable. What changes is who reads them. The design becomes your review surface; requirements become its annotation.

The proposalfive rules
D1The design artifact is your review surface. Requirements are never sent to you for review.

You review screens, states and flows. REQUIREMENTS.md stays exactly as it is and is read by the test engineer, the gate and the auditor — not by you.

D2Every requirement binds to something visible, or is marked no-surface.

The design engineer makes the binding: each numbered requirement names the screen, state or control where it becomes visible. Reviewing the design then reviews the requirements without reading them. A requirement that binds to nothing is explicitly marked, and those never reach you at all — they are the gate's and the auditor's business.

D3An escalation is shown, never described.

"R7 contradicts proposal decision 3" becomes here is the screen, here are the two versions, pick one — rendered, with buttons. If a conflict cannot be shown as a visible difference, it is not yet a question worth putting to you; it goes back to L2.

D4A demo before the ticket closes — with navigation.

Your ask, and the missing bookend: there is already a demo before a proposal is approved and nothing before a ticket is closed. Before you are asked to close, the session walks you to it — click by click, in the app's own words, on the built copy — showing what changed and what to look at. Closing without a demo is closing on trust, and it is the one decision where the evidence is cheapest to produce.

D5If it can't be shown, the design engineer's job is to find a way to show it — before falling back to words.

Rules, calculations and data policies feel invisible, but most of them surface somewhere: a number on a screen, an error state, an empty state, a disabled control. The design engineer looks for that surface first. Only when there genuinely isn't one may a question reach you as text — and then it is one sentence and two options, never a document.

What it costs, and what it won't fixstated
  • D2 is real work. Binding every requirement to a visible element is an extra pass for the design engineer, and it will sometimes reveal that a requirement can't be satisfied as specified — which is valuable, and still costs time.
  • no-surface is abusable. It is the obvious place to hide a decision you would have wanted to make. Two things push back: the proposal auditor already classifies silent decisions as yours, and D5 puts the burden on finding a surface first.
  • A demo at close costs a build and a walk-through every ticket. For a one-line fix that is disproportionate — the tier system should govern it, same as everything else.
  • It does not make backend work visible. A data-migration rule or an encryption policy has no screen, and pretending otherwise would be worse than admitting it. Those stay with the gate, and the honest consequence is that you are trusting the gate on them.
Open questionsyours
Does the merge approval become a demo too? It is the fourth row of that table and still prose. One demo could serve both merge and close — or merge stays a technical decision on the gate's evidence, and the demo belongs only at close, where the question is "is this what I wanted".
Should a small fix still get a demo? Every ticket, or only feature-tier work? Skipping it for small fixes is cheaper and reintroduces exactly the "optional means skipped" problem we just fixed for research.
Who marks no-surface? The design engineer who couldn't find a surface, or the requirements engineer who wrote it. The first knows more; the second has less incentive to use it as an escape hatch.
