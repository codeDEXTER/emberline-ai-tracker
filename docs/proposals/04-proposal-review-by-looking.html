Three looks, and the AI decides the rest

Amended 2026-08-04 after review. The first draft made your decisions visual. You asked for something stronger: make them fewer. You review three designs per task — after research, after design, after build — and the AI decides everything that doesn't change what those three look like.

What the first draft got wrong

It fixed the format of your decisions and left the volume alone. Your words: "majority of the decisions should be taken by the AI — that's why we have created this framework." And on pockets: "it's asking me for some random proposal two three one two and what decisions to be taken… I cannot take all the decisions on the requirements." Everything built today added decision points. This removes them.

The one that should worry us most is the last row

Closing a ticket is the moment you declare the thing you asked for actually exists. Right now nothing is shown to you at that moment — not a screen, not a demo, not a diff. The decision with the least evidence behind it is the one that ends the work.

This is the fourth time this pattern has come up, and ASKS.md already predicted it: "Show him the thing; don't describe the thing… one rule that keeps being rediscovered in a new context, which makes it an amendment candidate rather than a fourth independent statement of it." This proposal is that amendment — stated once, for all five decisions, instead of a fifth special case.

Why requirements are the wrong thing to send younot a preference — a role mismatch

REQUIREMENTS.md is written to be checkable by someone who wasn't in the conversation — that is its stated job. Its readers are the test engineer and the gate, and for them numbered, exhaustive, unambiguous prose is exactly right.

You are not that reader. You already know what you asked for. What you need to know is whether what got built looks like what you meant — a question prose answers badly and a screen answers instantly. Sending you the requirements is asking you to simulate a screen in your head from a list, which is slow, error-prone, and something the design artifact already does properly.

So: requirements don't go away, they stop being addressed to you

They are still written, still numbered, still testable — they are the contract the gate holds the work to, and removing them would remove the only thing that makes "built" checkable. What changes is who reads them. The design becomes your review surface; requirements become its annotation.

The proposal — three looksthis is the whole of your involvement in a task

One task, three moments where something is shown to you. Nothing else reaches you unless it changes what one of these three looks like.

LookWhenWhat you seeThe question it answers
LOOK 1After research, before requirements exist A design of how things would be modified — the change drawn on the screens it touches, alternatives where they exist Is this the right change?
LOOK 2After requirements and design, before code The buildable design — every state, with the modifications marked against what is there today Is this what I meant?
LOOK 3After the build, before the ticket closes The running thing, walked through click by click on the clean copy Is this what I got?

LOOK 1 replaces reading a research write-up. LOOK 2 replaces reading requirements and L2 escalation prose. LOOK 3 is the demo before close, which today does not exist at all.

The rulessix · the first is the one that matters
D1The AI decides by default. A decision reaches you only if it changes one of the three looks.

This is the reversal. Until now every ambiguity, conflict and drift classification was a question for you. Now the test is single and checkable: would answering this differently change what he sees at LOOK 1, 2 or 3? If no, the AI decides it, records it, and moves on. If yes, it becomes part of the next look — shown, not asked.

D2A decision the AI took can be reversed later through the normal issue path.

Your words: "take them, move ahead, and then I'll find the issue later." That is the trade D1 depends on, and it only works if reversal is cheap — so every decision the AI takes on your behalf is recorded where you can find it after the fact, against the issue, in one line. Not a document to read; a trail to search when something looks wrong.

D3Design is your review surface. Requirements are never sent to you.

REQUIREMENTS.md stays exactly as it is — it is the contract the gate holds the work to, and removing it would remove the only thing that makes "built" checkable. It is read by the test engineer, the gate and the auditor. Not by you.

D4Every requirement binds to something visible, or is marked no-surface.

The binding is what lets D1 work: the AI can settle a requirement itself and you still see the consequence, because the requirement names the screen or state where it lands. A requirement binding to nothing visible is marked, and never reaches you.

D5Richer graphics, not more words — and text only where a picture genuinely cannot carry it.

Reports and looks carry real graphics: before-and-after screens, marked-up states, diagrams. Your example of the genuine exception is a good one — evaluating AI models has no screen, and pretending otherwise would be worse than admitting it. There, text is allowed, and it is short.

D6Buttons only where there is a real decision.

This supersedes a decision you took earlier today (proposal 01: "buttons appear even when there is nothing to decide yet, option 'noted' if need be"). That rule was right about ambiguity and wrong about volume — it is part of what produced the pockets experience of being asked about "some random proposal two three one two". Your words on review: "if there is nothing to say, then don't show me a button. Just do it." So: no prompt, no confirmation, no "noted" — the work proceeds and the session states plainly what it did.

Two agents or one?asked on review · recommendation: keep two, re-justify the split

The case for merging is real. requirements-engineer and design-engineer have identical tool grants — Read, Write, Grep, Glob — so unlike every other boundary in this system, the split enforces nothing structurally. It also creates a loop that exists only because of it (design finds undefined behaviour, back to requirements), and this file's own research note says full-ceremony pipelines reduced correctness for most models tested.

Keep two anyway, because the current justification is weak and yours is not. The rules justify the split as a handoff of artifacts — "requirements says what must be true, design makes it buildable." Your framing is sharper: it is a split between thinking and rendering. One agent expands the reasoning; the other acts on it without redoing it.

The failure that earns the split

Requirements written after a design are not requirements — they are a caption. One agent handed both jobs will draw first and back-fill criteria describing what it drew, and then the gate is checking the work against a description of itself. Two agents make that impossible: the criteria exist before anything is drawn, so they can actually constrain it.

This is a different argument from the one justifying the explorer/engineer split, which is about conflicting instincts — exploration wants provocation, specification wants consistency, and one prompt holding both goes timid. Requirements and design are not conflicting instincts; they are sequential ones. Both splits are right, for unrelated reasons, and the rules should say so rather than implying one pattern.

The requirements engineer's "ask rather than assume" line is the single biggest source of the behaviour you are objecting to. It is not a drafting slip — it was deliberate, and it was right before D1. It is wrong now.

What it costs, and what it won't fixstated, because this one has real downside
  • You will get decisions you disagree with. That is not a risk of this proposal, it is the deal: the AI decides, you find it later, you file an issue. D2 exists to make "later" cheap. If the recorded trail is thin, this becomes worse than what we have now.
  • Three looks per task is more artifacts, not fewer. The saving is in your reading, not in the work — LOOK 1 and LOOK 2 are real design passes the design roles have to produce.
  • D1's test is only as good as the AI's judgement of "changes a look". A decision quietly reclassified as invisible is exactly how the escape hatch gets used. The proposal auditor is the counterweight and it is not a strong one.
  • Backend work stays invisible. An encryption policy, a migration rule, a model choice — no screen exists and none can be invented. You are trusting the gate on those, and D5 admits it rather than dressing it up.
A conflict you should decide deliberately

D6 reverses proposal 01's "buttons even when there is nothing to decide", which you chose this morning against my recommendation — and your reasoning then was good: an absent prompt is ambiguous. The cost showed up later, in pockets. Both cannot be true at once; the amended position is that a look ends with a plain statement when there is nothing to decide, and a prompt only when there is.

Open questionsyours
Does every task get all three looks? A one-line fix does not need LOOK 1. Tying the number of looks to the ceremony tier is the obvious answer, and it reintroduces "optional means skipped" — the failure we fixed for research this morning.
Where does the AI's decision trail live? Against the issue, in the checklist, or a per-task file. It has to be searchable after the fact without being something you read up front.
Does merge approval survive as a separate decision? LOOK 3 happens before close. Merge currently sits between the gate and close as a third thing — it could fold into LOOK 3, leaving you two decisions per task instead of three.
