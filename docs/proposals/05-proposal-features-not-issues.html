This has a name, and the literature is unambiguousresearched, not asserted

Horizontal slicing

Splitting one capability along technical layers — backend, UI, data — instead of along what a person can actually do. The consequences named in the literature are the ones you are living: horizontal slices "can't deliver value without interaction or integration with other layers", they produce "integration issues and delayed feedback", and "managing dependencies between horizontally sliced features can be intricate, as teams need to coordinate the delivery of different slices."

The alternative, vertical slicing, cuts end-to-end — UI through to the data store — so each piece stands alone and is independently testable. Teams prefer it consistently, for exactly this reason.

And the coordination cost is arithmetic, not vibes

Brooks's law puts communication paths at n(n−1)/2. Six issues in flight is fifteen pairs that might collide — and you are the only node that sees all six, so every one of those fifteen resolves through you. That is not you being a bottleneck by temperament. It is the shape of the graph.

The counter-intuitive finding on WIP limits is the useful one: limiting how much runs in parallel increases throughput, because "smaller work items improve feedback timing, reduce coordination overhead, and help maintain steady flow", and fewer simultaneous items means less context switching and fewer handoffs stalling between roles.

The proposalfive sentences

1 · The feature is yours. The issues are the AI's.

You approve a feature and see it at the three looks. The AI decides how many issues it takes, creates them, sequences them, hands off between them, and closes them. You never trigger an issue and never referee one.

2 · Issues are cut vertically — never "backend for X" and "UI for X".

Every issue is something a person can do end to end, from screen to store. If two proposed issues cannot ship independently, they are one issue. This is the rule that would have prevented #30 and #31 entirely, and it costs nothing to apply at decomposition time.

3 · One issue in flight per surface. The rest queue.

Extending the per-surface rule already agreed. Four issues touching ledger.py were never four parallel tasks. The AI sequences them; you see a feature progressing, not a queue.

4 · Dependencies are recorded when the issues are created, not discovered when they collide.

If issue B needs issue A's work, that is written down at decomposition. The handoff is then a planned event the AI executes, not an accident you referee.

5 · The AI closes issues. You close features.

A feature closes when you have seen it working. Its issues close as they finish, silently. This is the one that reverses an existing rule — see below.

Does this need a new agent?recommendation: no

No — and the reason is worth stating, because the opposite is tempting. Decomposition, sequencing and cross-issue handoff are already the project manager's job by definition: it owns ceremony tier, relaying between roles, and escalation. It hasn't been doing this job because the rules gave every issue decision to you, so it had no authority to decompose anything. Adding a ninth agent to perform work the project manager is defined to do but forbidden from doing would treat the symptom and leave the cause.

The standard practice points the same way, once translated: the literature puts epic decomposition with the product owner and story refinement with the team. Your feature is the epic and it stays yours. The issues are the stories and they move to the team — which here is the project manager and its agents.

What is genuinely missing is not an agent — it is a view

Nothing today shows a feature and its issues together. whoelse shows file contention; the checklist shows items; GitHub shows issues. None of them shows "Desktop Clerk: four issues, two done, one blocked on the other, here is the surface it touches." That view is what would have made #30 and #31 obvious at a glance, and it is a small piece of tooling, not a new role.

This proposal reverses all four of those for issues, and keeps all four for features. That is a deliberate trade and worth being explicit about what you give up: you stop seeing work arrive in units you approved individually, and you start trusting a decomposition you did not review. The protection is that the feature is still yours at three looks, and a bad decomposition shows up there as a feature that does not work.

It also interacts with the accepted rule that an accepted-but-unbuilt proposal resurfaces: under this proposal the AI creates its issues immediately on acceptance, so the gap that rule was written to catch mostly stops occurring.

What it costsstated
  • A bad decomposition is now invisible to you until a look. That is the whole trade. Vertical slicing is the mitigation and it is a rule, not a guarantee.
  • Fewer issues in flight means slower apparent progress. The board will look less busy. The research says throughput improves anyway; it will not feel that way at first.
  • 25 open issues do not re-cut themselves. The existing board was sliced horizontally and this proposal does not fix it retroactively — that is separate work, and the Desktop Clerk four are the obvious first candidate.
  • Closing issues without you means the audit trail matters more, and that trail is the same one proposal 04 depends on and which does not exist yet.
